Sample Lesson Plan for "Orbital Mechanics", Teen Level

Item Content
Grade Level(s) 10-12
Learning Objectives Understand the basics of orbital mechanics.
Prerequisites Basic algebra and geometry.
Duration n minutes
00:45. Watch the video Watch the video, "Space Flight: The Application of Orbital Mechanics."
00:45. Guided discussion Points to bring out:
  • Terms: orbit, ellipse, apogee, perigee, mass, square, cube, directly proportional, inversely proportional, velocity, acceleration, escape velocity, drag, major axis, semi-major axis, coordinate system, x-y plane, equatorial plane, orbital plane, vernal equinox, node, ascending node, descending node, line of nodes, prograde orbit, retrograde orbit, polar orbit, ground track, precession, posigrade burn, retrograde burn, delta V, transfer, thrust vector, field of view, geosynchronous orbit, geostationary orbit, molniya orbit, sun-synchronous orbit (highly inclined retrograde orbits), orbital perturbations
  • Kepler's 1st Law: Two bodies orbit in an ellipse circling two foci
  • Kepler's 2nd Law: Law of Areas: The line connecting a planet with the sun sweeps over an equal area in equal time intervals
  • Kepler's 3rd Law: Law of Periods: The square of a planet's period of revolution is directly proportional to the cube of its mean distance from the sun
  • Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation: Any two objects in the universe attract each other with a force directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them
  • Newton's 1st Law of Motion: A body in motion will keep moving at the same speed and int the same direction unless acted upon by an external force
  • Newton's 2nd Law of Motion: If the sum of the forces acting on an object is not zero, the object will have an acceleration proportional to the magnitude and in the direction of the net force
  • Newton's 3rd Law of Motion: For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction
  • Orbital Elements: a = length of the semi-major axis (size of orbit); e = eccentricity (shape of orbit - zero to 1); i = inclination; [omega] right ascension of the ascending node; [W] argument of perigee; tp = time of perigee passage
  • Geocentric Equatorial Coordinate System. Non-rotating coordinate system. Origin: Earth's center. X Y Plane: Earth's equatorial plane. Positive x axis points to the vernal equinox. Z axis is along the Earth's spin axis toward the north pole. Nodes are points in a satellite's orbit that intersect the Earth's equatorial plane. Asccending node = point at which satellite crosses equator going south to north. Line of nodes: Line joining the ascending and descending nodes.
  • Right Ascension of the Ascending Node: Angle between the x axis and the ascending node, always measured eastward in the direction away from the vernal equinox in the Earth's equatorial plane.
  • Argument of Perigee: Angle between the ascending node and the point of perigee; measured in the orbital plane in the direction of satellite motion.
  • Inclination: Angle between the equatorial plane and the orbital plane.
  • Prograde Orbit: Satellite has an eastward velocity component at the ascending node; has an inclination between 0 and 90 degrees.
  • Retrograde Orbit: Satellite has a westward velocity component at the ascending node; has an inclination between 90 and 180 degrees.
  • Polar Orbit: Satellite moves due north at the ascending node; inclination is exactly 90 degrees
  • Ground Track: Projection of satellite's path against a 2D map of the Earth's surface.
  • Precession: Motion of the orbital plane around the Earth's axis. Caused by Earth's rotation and the equatorial bulge. Toward the west for a prograde orbit and toward the east for a retrograde orbit.
  • Minimal orbital inclination is equal to the latitude of the launch site and is achieved by launching due east.
  • Launch Azimuth: Direction of flight at launch. Northward (increased) from due east increases the orbital inclination. Latitude limits of the ground track equal the new launch inclination. If decreased from due east orbital inclination also increases the orbital inclination and the latitude limits of the ground track.
  • Maximum practical inclination from Canaveral is 57 degrees, to prevent rockets from flying over land masses during ascent.
  • Vandenberg Air Force Base in California - max 70 degrees prograde to 138 degrees retrograde. Polar orbits are possible.
  • Velocity gain from Earth's rotation - Canaveral (28.5 deg lat) 1300 feet per second, Kourou 1500 feet per second (5 deg lat)
  • Launch Window factor. Lighting conditions, sun angle, payload orbit requirements, rendezvous phasing, tracking/communication requirements, collision avoidance.
  • Delta V: Incremental change in spacecraft velocity resulting from an on-orbit burn, measured in feet per second.
  • Posigrade Burn: Forward. Orbit is raised at all points except the burn point.
  • Retrograde Burn: Backward. Orbit is lowered at all points except the burn point.
  • Hohmann Transfer: Two-burn sequence. Minimal fuel, long time. One-half of an elliptical orbit with its perigee in one orbit and its apogee in the other, in the same orbital plane. Burns occur at the perigee and apogee of the transfer orbit.
