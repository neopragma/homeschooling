Why Homeschool?

Dave Nicolette

If you are considering homeschooling, are you clear about the reasons? Ask yourself what problem(s) you seek to solve or what specific goal(s) you hope to achieve by handling your children's education directly. Homeschooling is not as easy as some people assume, and if they are not doing it for strong reasons they may not have the stamina or perseverance to maintain proper attention to their homeschooling program.

The focus of this material is the United States in the 2020s. Some of this may be generally applicable, but on the whole it is specific to that country and time.

Parents who choose homeschooling are taking on a significant challenge. Even if they have just one child, taking the student from kindergarten through high school is a 13-year journey. Even if they can accelerate by 1 or 2 years, it's still a long commitment.

They have opted to homeschool instead of (a) public schools, (b) private and charter schools, (c) online schools, and (d) home-based or third-party supplementary education (tutoring, religious classes, etc.) on top of one of the first three options. Homeschooling demands more time, effort, focus, consistency, and long-term commitment from parents than any of the other available options for education.

If you can't articulate the reasons you're considering homeschooling, and you aren't pretty clear about what you're getting into, then it may be too soon for you to make a final decision about homeschooling.

I don't mean to discourage you from homeschooling; in fact I favor it strongly, given the general conditions in the United States these days. But if you aren't prepared to give it your full attention for the long haul, your children will only end up back in public school eventually, and they will almost certainly be behind the other students in their age group, specifically because you didn't give it your full attention while you were doing it.

It's my observation that homeschoolers in the United States choose that path for one or more of the following reasons:

  • to provide higher quality education than they feel is offered by public or private schools;
  • to ensure the safety of their children in a society characterized by bullying, school shootings, and carelessness about infectious diseases;
  • to provide supplemental content about a particular religious tradition, culture, or nationality;
  • to replace a curriculum they feel indoctrinates their children with values contrary to their own; and/or
  • to avoid the level of authority over their children asserted by government-run education systems, which they feel intrude upon their role as parents.

Quality of education

The general quality of education provided by US public schools is mixed. Some of the reasons for the variability are:

  • educational standards
  • variable funding
  • teacher pay
  • teacher unions
  • educational fads
  • the establishment clause
  • political socialization
  • propaganda
  • the textbook industry
  • class sizes
  • looping
  • diversion of attention and resources

Educational standards

In the US, public education is governed at the State level instead of the national level. There is great variation between different States regarding educational standards. The majority of public schools meet their State's standards to the letter, and do not go beyond. A high school graduate from a school in a State with high standards exhibits considerably more knowledge and ability than a graduate from a State with low standards.

The variation in standards is a hard problem to solve. It is exacerbated by a kind of domino effect, or what Systems Thinkers call a reinforcing loop. Low standards lead to graduates who are ill-prepared to meet the challenges of the modern world. That in turn leads to workers who are ill-prepared to perform jobs required by modern society. That in turn leads to states with relatively weak or sluggish economies. That in turn leads to low budgets for state-funded education. That in turn leads to poorly-performing schools. That in turn leads to lowered standards to make educators and students feel better about themselves. And that brings the state back around full circle. Each pass around the circle lowers standards still further. Generation by generation, as the population of that state becomes ever more ignorant, the people become less able to solve - or even to recognize - the problem.

Variable funding

Funding for schools generally comes from local taxes - either property tax or school tax, depending on locale. Affluent school districts have more money to work with than poorer districts. There is a correlation between the amount of money spent per student per year and general educational outcomes. A high school graduate from an affluent school has a stronger start in adult life than a graduate from a poorer school.

The socio-economic status of parents exacerbates the problem of uneven funding. Affluent school districts are usually populated by well-paid professional or white-collar people who tend to purchase their own homes. When referenda come up to approve additional taxes for education, they vote yes in support of their own children. Poorer districts are usually populated by renters. They pay property tax indirectly through their rent. The property owners are not interested in funding the school system, so they vote no to referenda to increase taxes for schools.

Teacher pay

School teachers are not highly respected in US culture. Taxpayers try to minimize their tax burden. Taken together, those two factors result in low pay for teachers. Many earn less per year than a minimum-wage worker at a fast food joint. This reflects the value Americans (in the aggregate) place on education for their children. Of course, you (on the individual level) place a high value on your own children's education, as demonstrated by the fact you are looking for information about homeshooling.

Due to the low pay and disrespect they must endure, many people who have other options do not choose to go into education as a career. Of those who do, many are really not the sort of people you would want influencing your child all day long. They are people who have no choice but to accept work as a teacher. They are not dedicated to it; it isn't their life's mission. While failing to educate your children in class, they also serve as role models of people who do the minimum they can get away with in their jobs.

Homeschooling will, at least, remove your child from this system. Even if you are not an expert educator, at least you do care about your child; and that in itself will take them farther than you might imagine.

Teacher unions

Teacher unions help protect teachers from arbitrary treatment by their employers, just as all labor unions protect workers. Unfortunately, unions are necessary because many employers will not act appropriately toward their employees unless coerced. Schools are no different from other employers in this regard.

However, teacher unions in the United States have become so powerful that it is all but impossible to remove or discipline an incompetent public school teacher. The purpose of the union is to protect teachers' jobs, not to ensure your children receive a good education. Unions can bring legal pressure to bear against parents who complain about teachers, and most parents lack the financial resources to counter such pressure.

Educational fads

Academics and practitioners in the field of education are constantly researching and trying out new and different ways to teach. They are sincerely interested in improving the state of the art of teaching. However, some of the ideas they implement do not work out well. Two fads in particular became popular in the latter half of the 20th century - new math and deconstructionism.

New math resulted in a generation of Americans who had little skill in mathematics and even less enthusiasm for it. Deconstructionism, as understood and used by public school teachers generally (as opposed to the level of understanding by university-level specialists), resulted in a generation of Americans who had no appreciation for literature, no interest in reading for pleasure, and little ability to apply critical thinking.

Today's teachers are one or two generations removed from that, and were raised in an environment where those things were considered normal. Many of them have no frame of reference even to perceive the problem, let alone fix it. If you suggest the quality of public education is low, they will simply become offended. They are not old enough to remember how mathematics and literature were taught before new math and deconstructionism.

Many fads come and go in the fields of psychology, sociology, and pedagogy. One example that is currently affecting the quality of education in the US is the widespread belief in the idea that each student has a preferred learning style, such as verbal, visual, or kinesthetic. When this idea became popular decades ago, people believed it meant that each student can learn best if they use their preferred learning style.

Subsequent research found that people's general aptitude for learning is about the same regardless of which approach they take. Someone who learns well verbally also learns well visually or kinesthetically. Someone who has difficulty learning will not overcome that difficulty simply by switching to a different approach.

That is not to say we should avoid using multiple approaches in teaching; it only means the idea of a rigid, inherent preference for one or another learning style is false. It was debunked as long ago as 2004. Yet, in recent surveys of practicing teachers, researchers found the vast majority of teachers still believe in this theory.

The establishment clause

The establishment clause in the first amendment to the US Constitution has been cited as a reason to omit any mention of religion in public schools, regardless of context. People worry that a particular religion will be elevated above others and promoted to the students, in violation of the Constitutional prohibition against establishing an official national religion.

To avoid criticism, schools take care never to mention religion at all. This goes well beyond Constitutional requirements. The strategy may indeed help schools avoid criticism, but religion is a core part of every human culture that has ever existed. Misunderstanding of and conflict between religions are at the heart of many of the world's problems today.

Is it possible for students to learn history, philosophy, ethics, economics, psychology, or a range of other subjects in the humanities without understanding the role of religion in society? It seems to me this is all the more important in a diverse, pluralistic society in which many religions are practiced.

This is a positive function of the public educational system in any country. Nation-states whose populations do not respect or care about their own country tend to become fragile, and are susceptible to economic, social, and political collapse.

Class sizes

The teacher-student ratio in most public and private schools is in the range of 1:25 to 1:45. The students in each class may come from different cultural, national, religious, linguistic, and economic groups; may have very different home lives; and may have a range of learning difficulties and special needs. Even the best teachers cannot guide every one of those students appropriately, especially while trying to keep the class as a whole moving along through the prescribed curriculum at the prescribed pace.

In contrast, a typical homeschool setup may have between 1 and 8 students who come from between 1 and 3 households representing a single demographic group. It is much easier for parents to work one-on-one with each child and to provide the level of focus necessary to guide them in their educational journey, even if the parent lacks the professional qualifications of a certified teacher. Homeschoolers need not force their "class" to move at a standard speed through standard material; they are free to allow students with particular interests to move ahead in those subjects, and to take more time with students who struggle with certain subjects.

Looping

You might remind me that two highly successful national public educational systems, Finland and Estonia, often have class sizes of around 30 students. There are a couple of salient differences between those systems and US schools. First, there is an explicit focus on encouraging individual interests in students. This has been shown to improve general learning, including in subjects that are not the individual's personal favorites.

Second, there is the practice of "looping." That means the same teacher follows a class through multiple years of education. In US schools, a teacher remains at the same grade level and takes on new students each year. With looping, a teacher gets to know each student well enough to help them learn effectively over multiple grades.

This is not necessarily a characteristic of Finnish or Estonian culture; when looping was tried in North Carolina, student achievement was higher than in traditional schools. Unfortunately, this was a short-term measure to deal with budget shortfalls in a small percentage of North Carolina schools, and not a strategy to improve academic outcomes. Yet, with looping only through two successive grades, student outcomes were notably improved.

As a homeschooler, you will "loop" along with your students throughout their basic education. So, an automatic benefit of homeschooling as compared with typical US schools is that the teacher follows the students through multiple grades. This in itself results in measurably better outcomes, even if the homeschooler is not a certified teacher.

As a homeschooler, you don't have to deal with all that. Your attention and resources are fully engaged with your own learners.

The argument in favor of using AI to route buses is that too many potential routes are possible for the human mind to grasp. The problem with this reasoning, in my view, is that we don't need to grasp every possible route as an abstract mathematical problem. We just need to run the buses around a relatively short circuit twice a day, through the neighborhoods served by a particular school.

The problems with AlphaRoute do not rise to the level of a decision point for home schooling, but we should consider the impact of the current trend to inject AI into everything from toasters to military strategy. The problem isn't the use of AI in situations where it adds value. The problem is the pervasive belief that AI is the answer to literally every problem. Americans seem to want to surrender their autonomy and reason to machines and algorithms. It's as if functioning as a human being is just too hard. As the trend continues, it is likely to have unpredictable negative effects on public services such as education. It also seems likely the educational system will inculcate the same mentality in the next generation of children, to the detriment of the entire country.

Religion and culture

Members of minority cultures and/or minority religions may choose homeschooling as a way to ensure their children learn about their own history and gain a sense of pride in their own heritage. In most cases, parents can achieve this by supplementing public or private schools with additional studies, either at home or through a community or religious organization.

There are private schools representing particular nationalities and religions where parents can send their children for supplementary culture-specific education or religious indoctrination outside of standard public school hours. Chinese schools, Hebrew schools, and many others teach language, culture, and history to supplement the standard State curriculum. Christian churches often offer Bible study groups and scripture lessons for children, typically after Sunday services each week. There are many options.

Some parents may want a more immersive cultural or religious educational experience for their children than supplemental classes can provide. There are comprehensive, accredited private schools that provide a religious education, but they tend to be expensive and there may be no such school near a family's residence. Parents may opt for homeschooling to provide the cultural or religious focus they want, without the expense of a private school.

As we are focused on the United States, three groups in particular may find homeschooling the best option to provide this sort of educational experience for their children:

  • Black Americans
  • Native Americans
  • White Evangelical Christians

Black Americans and Native Americans have unique historical experiences with respect to the United States, and parents may want to provide education that is centered on their culture and on how to thrive and succeed in America. There is virtually no chance of getting that sort of cultural focus or targeted support in a mainstream public school in the US.

The third special group may be surprising - fundamentalist or evangelical Christians. In a country where around 80% of people self-identify as Christians, where around 75% of the population is of white European ancestry, and where public school curricula present a European, Christian worldview across the board, the need for a special Christian educational program may not be apparent.

American Christians span a wide range of "flavors" of Christianity, from Orthodox to Catholic to multiple forms of Protestantism. Fundamentalist Christians have a particular concept of Christianity and its role in the country and society. Many of them do not consider the mainstream Christian worldview to represent their own fundamentalist Christian values.

Values

Many public school officials and functionaries behave as if they believe our children belong to them and not to the parents. They will dictate what information is presented at what age level and in what manner. Many educators believe their mission in life is to "fix the country" by changing the cultural values that children learn at home.

When parents disagree, they run the risk of having the power of state government come down on them, even to the extent of losing custody of their children. Public schools, as tax-funded entities, ought to function as partners with parents to help raise well-functioning adult members of society. If they cannnot or will not function in such a way, then parents may choose to homeschool.

I am not interested in criticizing or judging anyone's values, but I must observe that many Americans are frustrated and disappointed in the values they feel public schools are teaching their children. Americans who are looking at the very same school and the very same curriculum may see two quite different problems.

One group thinks schools are indoctrinating the children to be mindless "patriots" who wave the flag and follow orders, but who lack a realistic or complete understanding of US history. The other group thinks schools are indoctrinating the children to hate their own country and to believe it is inferior to the rest of the world.

Whether one is right, or both are right depending on how you look at it, I can't say definitively. I doubt such extreme views represent objective reality, in any case. However, this is a reason many parents choose homeschooling. They want to be in control of the values that are presented to their children.

Bureaucratic requirements

The US in the early 2020s is in an unusual state - there's a pandemic underway, and people have grown tired of the adjustments they've had to make to cope with the risk of infection. In the section about safety (above), I mention the issue of the children's safety against infection when the public schools do not enforce health measures such as vaccinations and face masks.

In this section, I'm going to offer the opposite point of view as a reason to homeschool. This may seem contradictory, but in fact both perspectives are common and different parents may see the situation in either of these two ways.

In the school board protests by conservative activists in the early 2020s, one of the signs carried by a concerned parent read, "We don't co-parent with the government." This reflects the attitude that the parents have primary responsibility for raising their children, and the government (represented by public schools) ought to function in a supportive role rather than in the primary role.

Many of these parents believe the virus is not dangerous, or is just a hoax, or that masks are not effective, or something along similar lines. They resent the government telling them that they must get a vaccine or that they must wear face masks.

If you are among these parents, and you agree with the sentiment that you don't co-parent with the government, then this may be a strong reason for you to consider home schooling.

Update 22 August 2023: The pandemic is over, although the Coronavirus is still around, having become part of the environment we live in. The pattern of bureaucrats asserting control of your children, superseding your natural authority as parents, is the deeper problem. That is the reason to consider home schooling. The problem did not go away when the pandemic faded.

Closing thoughts

The point of this page is to summarize the most common reasons parents choose homeschooling over other educational options. If you feel there are sufficient reasons for you to consider homeschooling, then the next step is to read the opinions of those who oppose homeschooling. They have valid points that you should consider before making your final decision.

There are "horror stories" about homeschooling. Probably the worst is the situation where parents keep their kids home ostensibly for religious reasons, but they lock up their little girls until they start to menstruate, and then sell them to older pedophiles for "marriage" in exchange for a "dowry." These parents use the homeschooling laws of their states as a cover for this activity.

A much more common situation is that a graduate of a homeschooling program enters the workforce barely able to read and write, and with so little general knowledge that they can't stay employed for long. They don't know how to fill in a job application, rent an apartment, pay their taxes, or manage a household budget. Their parents kept them home from school but did not fill in the gap - they never properly homeschooled the kids.

That is the reason I say you need to take this decision seriously and be very sure you understand your own reasons for doing it, the legal procedures to enable it, and the level of personal commitment and effort you are signing up for. Even if there are some things about public schools you disagree with, you might conclude that you can deal with those issues more effectively than you can handle the full burden of educating your children yourself.
