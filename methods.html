Pedagogical Methods

This section contains links to information about pedagogical methods you might employ in homeschooling. These methods are touted to provide better results with less stress and a smaller investment of your time than traditional classroom teaching methods.

The traditional classroom teaching approach used in most schools involves lecture by a teacher reinforced by homework carried out independently of the teacher. The emphasis is on memorization of the information presented. For the most part, different subject areas are taught in isolation, so students have difficulty perceiving a larger context. In addition, work is carried out by each student individually, without collaborating with other students. Those factors reduce the effectiveness of traditional methods.

The methods described below emphasize learner-directed and self-paced learning with guidance by a teacher. Learning crosses the boundaries of various disciplines and connects the dots between them to provide students with a cohesive and holistic view of the world. Memorization of basic facts still plays a role in learning, but there is a greater emphasis on critical thinking and practical application of information. These methods also encourage collaboration among learners. This general approach has demonstrated better outcomes for learners in less time than the traditional classroom lecture approach.

Modified Socratic Method

Helps build engagement and participation as well as helping to foster independent thinking. Socrates developed his method for an audience of knowledgeable students who were already at an advanced level of study. O'Hara has modified this approach to work with people who do not already have much knowledge of the subject area they are studying.

Deliberate Practice and Deliberate Play

Deliberate practice, also known as mindful practice, involves setting specific goals for each practice session and focusing on the systematic development of skills necessary to reach that goal. It was developed as an improvement over the notion of "10,000 hours of practice," which may involve little more than mindless repetition of patterns. Deliberate Play involves crafting play scenarios that build on deliberate practice as a way to side-step learners' hesitation or embarrassment about underperforming at an unfamiliar task. Through a combination of the two, we can maximize the value of the available learning time.

Project-based Learning

With Project-based Learning (PBL), students engage in real-world projects that may take weeks or longer to complete. In the course of working on a project, students try to solve a real problem or address a genuine need. In the course of doing so, they learn various skills and acquire knowledge relevant to the project.

PBL is most effective when multiple students work collaboratively. The approach has been shown to build cognitive skills, problem-solving skills, and interpersonal skills. In a homeschool setting, PBL would probably be used in conjunction with other pedagogical methods. It would not be the sole mechanism for learning.

Spaced Repetition

Spaced Repetition is a study technique in which we review the same material repeatedly. Each review is short and is followed by a break - some period of time when we do something else, study something else, rest, or play. The intervals between review sessions gradually become longer.

The technique was first promoted by Hermann Ebbinghaus in the 1880s. Originally, it was intended as a memory-building method. Ebbinghaus proposed the idea of the "forgetting curve," which posits that we will forget things pretty quickly unless we refresh our memory periodically. After each periodic refresher, we forget a little less than before.

There are various opinions about the specific time intervals between review sessions for maximum effectiveness. To an extent, this seems to be related to the nature of the material we're trying to learn; for instance, memorizing some historical dates or memorizing the multiplation tables is one kind of learning, and learning a foreign language is another, with each calling for different review intervals.

Subsequent research and experience has shown that Spaced Repetition works well in conjunction with Deliberate Practice when our goal is to acquire a new skill, as opposed to memorizing facts. Thus a combination of Deliberate Play, Deliberate Practice, and Spaced Repetition can help with building a skill like playing a musical instrument or making pottery.

Be advised there a are great many Internet resources devoted to Spaced Repetition, and most of them are either hard to understand, incomplete, or just plain wrong - including many that are hosted on the websites of educational institutions or training companies. Caveat surfer!

Explain a Subject in 5 Levels of Difficulty

In a series of challenges documented in videos, experts in various fields have been asked to explain a subject for five different audiences: Child, Teen, College Student, Grad Student, and Expert. If you can explain a topic in your homeschool curriculum at multiple levels of difficulty - say, Child, Adolescent, and Teen - the overall effectiveness of your program may be improved. This approach works for any subject. You might get some ideas from these videos.
