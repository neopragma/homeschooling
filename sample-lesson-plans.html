Lesson Plans

There's no "correct" or "standard" lesson plan format, and the level of detail depends on how you intend to use the plan. Will it be a reminder to you of the learning objectives you want to cover, or will it be a hands-on guide for the students to follow step by step?

If the former, the lesson plan can be as simple as a checklist of topics or keywords. If the latter, it probably should be much more detailed than that, and written with the age of the students in mind.

Lesson Plan Considerations

While there's no standard format, lesson plans need to have certain elements if they are to be useful.

Learning objective

Probably the most important piece of information is the learning objective: What do you want the student to know or be able to do at the end of this lesson that they don't know at the beginning?

Starting with the learning objective will help you craft the rest of the lesson plan. It's the old idea to "begin with the end in mind."

It's probably a good idea to limit the number of objectives per lesson to between 1 and 3 distinct things. If you find yourself listing more than 3 learning objectives, it might be a sign that you should break up the topic into more and smaller lessons. This is another advantage of starting with the learning objective: It helps you stay focused on the scope and purpose of each lesson, and avoid making the lessons too long or complicated.

Prerequisites

For any given lesson, we expect the students to know something already. The purpose of a lesson is to take the student from some point in their learning journey to some other point. What do they need to know in order to understand and follow the lesson plan we're writing?

The prerequisites may depend on the age group or grade level of the students. For example, the sample lesson plans for Orbital Mechanics are written at three levels: Child, Adolescent, and Teen. The Child plan doesn't require any math. The Adolescent plan requires pre-algebra. The Teen plan requires basic algebra and geometry.

Learning styles

Many professional educators and researchers hold that people have different learning styles, either as a matter of their inherent makeup or as a matter of personal preference. Some researchers discount this. In any case, it can be helpful for students to access the material in more than one way.

Using conventional labels as a general model, even if some researchers question the details, it might be a good idea to include activities in each lesson plan that cater to textual learners, visual/audio learners, and kinesthetic learners. There's also value in using games as a tool for learning.

Different learning objectives may lend themselves to different learning styles more easily than others. For instance, the topic of "Replace a Lockset On an Interior Door" lends itself naturally to kinesthetic learning activities - hands-on.

It's more challenging to come up with activities about "Orbital Mechanics" for kinesthetic learners. It isn't practical to send your kids into space to practice orbit transfers hands-on. In our sample lesson plans, we use a video game called Kerbal Space Program for the Adolescent and Teen lesson plans, and an arts and crafts activity for the Child lesson plan.

Teaching methods

Your lesson plans may be different depending on the teaching methods you intend to use. If you use conventional lecture-style teaching, the lesson plan is really for your use, to guide you in your presentation. If you use Flipped Learning, the lesson plan will have to be designed for students to use independently; you might want to prepare a separate checklist of items for yourself, so you won't forget how to prepare and facilitate the lesson. If you're using a Montessori set up, the lesson plan will have to be usable by older students to help younger ones.

Time

It's helpful if each lesson has a defined time for completion. Teachers who work in educational institutions have to craft their lessons to fit into fixed time slots - often about 45-50 minutes for high school and 60-75 minutes for college. As a homeschooler, you're not constrained in that way. You could have one lesson that takes a couple of hours and another that takes several days to complete. But in any case, defining a time span for the lesson will help you keep the lesson plan focused.

Materials and supplies

If you have to prepare for the lesson in any way, it's a good idea to note that in your lesson plan so you won't forget. Ultimately, you'll have hundreds of lesson plans, and you won't automatically remember every detail.

For our sample lesson plan, "Replace a Lockset on an Interior Door," there's no Child version, and the Adolescent version simply has the students remove and replace the same lockset on a door. Probably the only preparation you'll need is to make sure you have a screwdriver of the right type and size on hand. For the Teen version, we have the student install a lockset on a new interior (hollow core) door. So, you'd need the new lockset and any additional tools necessary to make sure the holes and cutouts in the door line up with the new lockset.

For our sample lesson plan, "Orbital Mechanics," the Child version has arts-and-crafts activities, while the Adolescent and Teen versions use a commercial video game. All three versions include watching videos online. Preparation for the Child version includes having the materials on hand for the activity, such as poster board, construction paper, safety scissors, crayons or markers, paste or glue, and pictures of planets, moons, and spacecraft cut from magazines. Preparation for the Adolescent and Teen versions includes making sure you have the game downloaded or you've subscribed to it on Steam, and advancing the game to the point that orbital mechanics come into play.

Assessments

If you want to have learners do a formal assessment of knowledge and skills, you'll need to prepare it beforehand. It could be a demonstration of skills, a written "test," or a discussion in which you ask particular questions. Whatever form the assessment takes, you should document it in your lesson plan.

Rewards

If you intend to give a reward for completing the lesson or unit, you'll need to make sure you have the physical or electronic rewards ready - stickers, ribbons, or graphics for students to post online.

Any such preparation you need to do should be documented in the lesson plan.

Sample Lesson Plans

Here are some sample lesson plans you can use for reference or to get ideas for how to write your own lesson plans. Some were intended as examples or templates, others are real lesson plans I found online, and some are lesson plans I wrote, following my own advice about presenting material at different levels of detail and providing multiple ways to approach the material, such as text-based, video, hands-on, and gameplay. I tried to find examples from different subject areas, like science, history, ethics, and household maintenance, so show how lesson plans might differ depending on the topic. These are in alphabetical order by title, and not organized by age, grade level, or subject. The key thing is to see different practical formats for lesson plans. I hope it's helpful.

  • Connecting with Kindness (Local copy)
    This is a Christian-focused lesson plan for young children, based on the affirmation, "The whole world is home, and we are a divine family." The lesson plan format provides all the useful kinds of content mentioned on this page, even if the author uses different words for some of the concepts. As a Christian lesson, it includes relevant Bible verses. There are different kinds of learning activities, including reading, discussing, and thinking and writing.
  • Orbital Mechanics
    This is a set of three sample lesson plans I wrote to illustrate how you might present the same subject at different grade levels. In this case, the levels are Child, Adolescent, and Teen rather than numerical grade levels. The format is somewhat ad hoc, but follows the advice offered on this page regarding the content of a lesson plan.
  • Replace a Lockset on an Interior Door
    This is a sample lesson plan for a unit about household maintenance. Household maintenance is not a common subject in public schools, but in my view is a necessary part of a person's preparation for adult life. Here we are dealing with a typical hollow-core door, usually found in houses built in the United States. The lesson can be tailored for Adolescent or Teen learners, with the Teens using power tools to prepare a new hollow-core door to take the lockset. The skills the learners will practice are applicable to other tasks in everyday life, as well as to work in the trades.
  • Strategies and Compromises: Women in Astronomy at Harvard College Observatory, 1870-1920 (Local copy)
    This is an actual lesson plan provided by the American Institute of Physics, meant to be part of a program about the history of science for grade levels 9-12. It exemplifies a lesson plan format known as Engage - Explore - Explain - Elaborate - Evaluate. For each of the five phases of the lesson, this format describes what the teacher does and why, and what the students do and why.
  • Verb Tenses: Past, Present, Future (Fourth Grade)
    This is an actual lesson plan provided by education.com (Local copy), a membership site that offers various resources for professional teachers, students, and homeschoolers. It exemplifies a very simple lesson plan format that lays out learning objectives, preparation and materials, and step by step teaching activities in a clear way.
