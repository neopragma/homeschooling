Rewarding Achievement

It helps learners to see their own progress and to receive encouragement for reaching milestones and completing learning objectives. A reward system will also help you remember where each child stands in each subject. It can be part of your general record-keeping system.

Example Reward System

Post a large placard on a wall that has each learner's name listed down the left-hand side and time shown across the top. As learners complete study units or learning objectives or whatever the measurement for progress is to be, add a sticker next to the learner's name. They can see their progress on the wall and take encouragement from it.

Another option is to give each learner a blank book and place the stickers there, so they can keep the record of their own progress after each term. You could also prepare custom printable sheets that correspond with your curriculum, and mark each learner's progress on the sheets, which they can also keep.

Tips for Rewards

Younger kids will enjoy colorful, tactile items while older kids will prefer record-keeping tools that feel more grown-up to them. You may need two to four "levels of grown-up-ness" in the rewards you give, depending on the ages of your students.

Younger kids will benefit from more-frequent rewards for smaller achievements than older kids need.

Younger kids usually think it is fair for everyone to receive the same reward for the same achievement. They often check to make sure a classmate has not received a different reward than they did for the same achievement. For older kids, you can consider the preferences of each child and give personalized rewards for larger achievements.

You can make custom stickers that correspond with the study units or activities or adventures or explorations or experiments (or whatever) that are in the curriculum - whatever the unit of work is that you want to track. For some purposes, a standard mass-produced classroom sticker serves well enough, especially to reward good behavior, sharing, collaboration, and so forth.

A custom sticker that specifically shows the learner has achieved a particular learning objective provides more meaning to them than a generic sticker. Rather than saying, in effect, "You're a good boy!" as if they were a dog, the sticker indicates the learner "completed Unit 12 in 5th Grade History" or they designed and carried out an "Experiment to Demonstrate the Gas Laws" or they "can play all the major and minor scales on the trumpet" or they "can do ten pull-ups" or they "can cut paper with scissors" - concrete achievements that they have attained through their own hard work.

The reward for achieving a major milestone, such as passing a state standardized test or completing an entire grade level, should be different from the rewards for smaller achievements, so they stand out. Achieving a major milestone could call for a framed certificate or a custom T-shirt or an online gift card or a meal at a favorite restaurant rather than just a sticker.

You can make custom stickers by using clipart found online and/or graphics software to customize printable stickers, certificates, and other items. Please see the Books and Supplies page for links to sources of printer-ready forms, labels, and stickers; sources of clipart and other images; software for creating and modifying images; and online services to help you create and print forms, certificates, labels, stickers, as well as "badges" the students can post on social media.

You will probably make use of many different online teaching/learning resources, including virtual classes and video classes. Many of these issue electronic "badges" for completion of courses or course modules. These can become part of your overall reward system.

Too Much or Too Little

A reward system can backfire. Two common errors are (a) to give meaningless rewards and (b) to withhold rewards unless and until the child performs perfectly, or until they have completed a substantial amount of work that takes more than a couple of days for younger kids, or more than a week or so for older ones.

Children are not fooled when they receive an award they know they do not deserve. Often, when a community center hosts a pet show for neighborhood children, they give every pet a ribbon; when a martial arts school that caters to families holds a tournament, every child receives a trophy or medal; in some school systems, every member of every school band or orchestra receives a ribbon or medal for participating in the annual music contest. Take care not to fall into this pattern.

Kids understand what a "participation trophy" or "self-esteem award" is, and they do not want them. They are badges of shame that prove they did not perform well. They sit on the child's shelf in their room, reminding them every night when they go to bed that they are merely mediocre. This will not help your child progress with homeschooling. It could even cause them to dislike school and resist learning.

At the other extreme, the attitude that "second place is the first loser" or "only straight A's will be tolerated" can be discouraging and harmful to children. They need fairly frequent positive reinforcement to keep their spirits up - probably more than adults do. School or homeschool is not a dog-eat-dog, cut-throat competition intended to leave just one child standing at the end of the school term.

The children are learning how to be fully functional adults - but they are not adults yet. They need to learn how to assess the results of the things they attempt and to learn from those results. It is not a question of "winning" or "losing." It is a question of teaching them how to learn from the outcomes they achieve, to avoid getting cocky when they are successful, and to pick themselves up and try again when things do not go well. They can "win" or "lose" after they have grown up, and they are prepared to handle those outcomes in a mature way.

If they are rewarded excessively, they may learn that winning is easy and expect to get real-world rewards without effort when they are grown. If they are not rewarded as they progress, they may learn that they will never be "good enough" and fail to reach their potential as adults. It is a balancing act.
