Commentary on "10 Effective Study Techniques"

Dave Nicolette

One of the resources we recommend to learn about effective study techniques is an unsigned editorial on the University of St. Augustine for Health Sciences website entitled, "10 Effective Study Techniques to Try This Year". All the advice in the article is excellent. I'd like to expand a little on some of the techniques mentioned in the article.

Caveat #1: Background music for studying

One of the suggestions in the "Set the Stage" section is Listen to calming music in the background during study periods. The intent is to help counteract distracting sounds in the environment.

If your learners are musically inclined, even if they are not studying music formally, do not play background music while they are studying. Studying music formally or informally from a young age alters the physical structure of the brain. Any sort of music, including "calming" music, will capture the person's attention. They will start to think about the structure of the music instead of the subject they are trying to study. There is nothing they can do to prevent this effect. For them, background music is a greater distraction than general ambient sounds.

Our caveat is consistent with the article's advice to Eliminate distractions. The article's intent is to use music to mask distracting ambient noise; but you must recognize the cases when background music is, itself, the distracting noise.

Upvote #1: Snacks

The suggestion to Snack on smart food is significant. Many private schools and homeschoolers, not to mention places of work where the management understands the positive effects of healthy snacks, provide snacks. Beyond the recommendation in the artle, we suggest you mindfully consider the specific foods you want your learners to consume and provide them with the portions you want them to eat at the times of day you choose. Apart from regular meals, the learners should not eat other things during the day.

Clarification #1: The Feynman Technique

Richard Feynman made many statements of the general form, "If you can't explain it to someone else in simple language, then you don't really understand it." The article lists "The Feynman Technique" as a specific study technique (item 5). It also lists "The PQ4R Method" (item 4), "Retrieval Practice" (item 2), and "The S3QR Method" (item 1).

All of these are variations on Feynman's idea. For instance, you can probably see how similar S3QR and PQ4R are:

S3QRPQ4RObservations
Survey Preview 2 words, same meaning
Question Question Why are you about to read this? What information are you looking for? What skill to you seek to cultivate?
Read Read  
  Reflect S3QR has no separate Reflect step, but reflection occurs while reading, so it is effectively the same as P4QR
Recite Recite Feynman's "Explain it in simple language"
Review Review Also Leitner - moving cards between boxes

I think you can see that S3QR and PQ4R can be understood as structured methods for putting Feynman's approach into practice. The Recite step is the same as "explaining to someone else in simple language," and if you're studying alone then you're explaining the subject to yourself. The Review step is the feedback from your attempt to explain the subject. It guides you in going back through the material to refine your knowledge. The feedback can come from someone else or from yourself.

"Retrieval Practice" is less structured than the other methods mentioned here, but it seems consistent with Feynman's approach. It can work in a group study setting or for individual study.

I've mentioned group study a couple of times now. Let me suggest that studying with one or more partners is far more effective than studying alone. Feynman's approach and any of the similar study techniques listed in the article work very well in study groups.

If you are teaching just one child at home, consider organizing study groups with other homeschoolers and/or with friends who attend regular schools. If you are teaching two children, one older than the other, the younger one can still provide value as a study partner for the older one. They don't have to be at the same grade level to help each other study effectively.

Clarification #2: Tools vs. Techniques

The article lists flashcards (item 6), color coding (item 7), and mind mapping (item 8) as study techniques. I think it makes sense to distinguish these as tools that you and your learners might use when applying one or more of the other techniques from the article, or from other sources.

For example, the act of making flashcards feels a lot like the Recite step in S3QR and PQ4R. Flashcards are mentioned in "Retrieval Practice" (item 2) and seem to me to be a form of "make your own questions" as well as "explain in your own words" under Feynman (item 5). Flashcards are also mentioned under Leitner (item 6). So, flashcards as such strike me less as a technique in their own right than as a tool we can use to implement any study technique we may choose.

Upvote #2: Study Before Bed

A big thumbs up for this suggestion. In many families, the custom is to have the children do their homework and study before they do anything else. The intent is to ensure they actually get around to finishing their homework at some point before bed time. That is understandable, but there are reasons why this may not be the best time for studying.

Your homeschool routine may differ from regular schools, so this point may not be relevant to you; but when children arrive home at the end of the school day, they are already tired and stressed. It's the same as coming home from a day at work. They need to do something other than school work for a while, to reset and recharge themselves.

At the same time, the general conditions in the household around bed time tend to be calmer and quieter than in the middle of the afternoon. In addition to the rationale given in the article, these are additional reasons to schedule regular study periods as the final activity of the day.

Another common practice in many households is to discourage the children from reading in bed after their official bed time. This is actually the best possible time for them to read. They will fall asleep naturally when their bodies tell them to. We have to strike a balance between the advice to get a good night's sleep and the advice to study just before going to sleep.

Caveat #2: Study Before Bed

I'm upvoting "Study Before Bed," but there's also a caveat. If your learner is on the attention deficit spectrum, it's likely they will be unable to maintain attention on school work for an extended time. They will need to break up their time and do different activities for shorter periods than other children. They probably need to have several short study periods per day rather than a single long period. Even then, they may not be able to study at the same time every day, depending on how they're feeling. Some flexibility is advised.

In addition to time considerations, also bear in mind many of these children think better on their feet than when they are struggling to sit still. Don't worry if they walk around the room, manipulate objects, or fidget. It's normal for them.

"Normal" for the majority is not the definition of "correct" or "mandatory." Forcing people to be something they aren't is not usually a constructive approach.

Consider this: No one who has founded a successful business or invented a remarkable new product did so because they were able to sit still for several hours at a stretch. It is not a useful skill...unless you are a classroom teacher facing 45 students for the next 6 hours. In that case, it's good for you if the kids can remain quiet. Even then, the ability to sit still all day is not a useful skill for the students.
