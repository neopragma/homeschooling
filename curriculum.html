Curriculum

What's a curriculum?

The Merriam-Webster Dictionary defines curriculum as (1) the courses offered by an educational institution, or (2) a set of courses constituting an area of specialization.

Many websites that offer curriculum advice for homeschooling parents provide just what the definition states - a list of subjects. That may or may not be sufficient to help you craft an educational experience that takes your children to the desired end point. Unless you are an experienced teacher already, you may nor may not be able to take a bare list of subject titles and compile them into a meaningful roadmap to meet your children's educational goals.

I suggest a practical definition of curriculum for homeschooling purposes is "a series of lesson plans and/or other learning activities carried out in a specified sequence from a defined starting point to achieve an educational goal."

If the goal is "satisfy Arizona's educational requirements for completing the fourth grade," then your fourth grade curriculum will not simply be a list of subjects, but will consist of a sequence of lesson plans designed to take your children from the end of third grade to the end of fourth grade, according to the standards of the State of Arizona, including preparation for standardized tests. Your curriculum will also include additional subjects and lesson plans to cover enriched learning that you intend to provide to your children, such as STEM activities beyond the fourth grade level, music and fine arts, religious indoctrination, culture-specific content, or anything else you want them to learn.

Is there a standard curriculum?

In the United States, there is no standard or legally-mandated curriculum for homeschooling, or for public education generally. However, each State has its own guidelines and requirements for content and for assessment testing at each grade level, as well as requirements for high school graduation. You should also consider admission requirements for universities where your children may wish to apply after completing high school, as these may involve additional learning beyond the State's minimum standards.

You must meet the State's minimum standards, but you are free to extend those standards in whatever way you wish. You might want to provide deeper instruction in some subjects than your State minimally requires. You might want to add vocational training, religious indoctrination, political indoctrination, or subjects not usually taught in conventional schools, such as ethics, philosophy, statistics, business, household management, classical languages, or topics related to a specific ethnic or national heritage. You can always add to, but never remove from, your State's educational requirements.

You can even contradict what the public schools teach, as long as you prepare your students to pass the State's standard assessment tests by providing whatever answers the State deems "correct."

What happened to the common core?

Some years ago, a group of professional educators proposed a set of core educational standards for the United States to help level out the widely-varying State-level standards. The idea met with strong resistance from people who feared it was a cover for the government to indoctrinate their children politically, or for the federal government to intrude into an area that had been reserved to the States.

The core standards were never formally adopted, but they still exist. They offer guidance regarding the skills a person needs to function well in adult society, focusing on two areas: English language and mathematics. They have no political content. They focus on skills for learning new information, conveying information effectively to others, and working with others.

As a homeschooler, the common core provides a useful resource to help prepare lesson plans. Each standard is defined for various grade levels or for college preparation. One example is standard CCSA.ELA-LITERACY.SL.3.1.B, a standard for "Comprehension and Collaboration" at the Grade 3 level. It reads, "Follow agreed-upon rules for discussions (e.g., gaining the floor in respectful ways, listening to others with care, speaking one at a time about the topics and texts under discussion)." This doesn't give us any specific advice about what to teach, but it does help us understand what is reasonable to expect from a student at the Grade 3 level.

Here's another example: CCSS.ELA-LITERACY.RST.11-12.7, a standard for "Integration of Knowledge and Ideas" at grade levels 11 and 12. It reads, "Integrate and evaluate multiple sources of information presented in diverse formats and media (e.g., quantitative data, video, multimedia) in order address a question or solve a problem." This says nothing about content, but does help us understand what skills a student at Grade levels 11 and 12 should have.

Clearly, these are not complete lesson plans or objectives in scope for any single lesson. Instead, they represent skill sets or capabilities recommended for students at a given grade level. As a homeschooler, when preparing your lesson plans, you can incorporate content and activities that touch on these skill sets in multiple lesson plans. As of this writing, no State requires curricula to adhere to the Common Core. It's an optional resource that may help you prepare lesson plans.

There is no political or religious content in any of these standards. They describe skills necessary for learning and for interacting with others effectively. As the homeschool parent, you decide on the content of lessons - whether you want to teach your children a balanced version of US history or a racist version; whether you want them to be Christians or Muslims or atheists. No matter what you want your children to learn, they will need these skills in language and math to learn it. So, there is no reason to fear the common core will contradict the messaging you want your children to receive.

Update 18 April 2022: The State of Florida has approved its own curriculum standards, called Benchmarks for Excellent Student Thinking, or B.E.S.T. The standards are very similar to the Common Core standards. While the Common Core is not required in any State, B.E.S.T. is mandatory in Florida. If you live in Florida, you should ensure your homeschool curriculum is aligned with B.E.S.T. so that your program will be accredited by the State. Find more information here and here.

Pre-built curricula

If you purchase a commercial curriculum package, review it thoroughly against your State's requirements. Education in the US is governed at the State level, and there are differences from State to State. A "canned" curriculum package will be generic, and may not contain everything your State requires for high school graduation. Expect to add content to fill in any gaps.

Some commercial curriculum packages cover all subjects and grade levels, but many are concerned with just one subject area or a limited range of grade levels. Be sure you have sufficient material to cover everything necessary for your children to pass the State's assessment tests. You will probably end up with a combination of commercial packages and self-created curriculum materials.

Also bear in mind many pre-built curricula will only consist of a list of subjects, and no lesson plans. They are curricula according to the dictionary definition, but they may not provide enough guidance for you. Check what the package includes and plan on supplementing it as needed.

Following a curriculum

Following a curriculum is not easy. Even if you purchase a pre-built curriculum prepared by expert educators, you will be covering multiple aspects of life learning and multiple academic subjects simultaneously. You will have to provide guidance and instruction appropriate to each age group you are teaching, as well as managing special needs children, all simultaneously and consistently over a period of years.

There are a lot of balls to juggle, even if you wrote the curriculum and the lesson plans yourself. When following other people's lesson plans, you will encounter many places where their intent or instructions are unclear, or where the information they provide is incomplete, or where you suddenly discover you need materials or resources that weren't obvious at first.

You also have to pay attention to the schedule. Most States require standardized tests at the end of certain grade levels. Your children may be ahead of the public school calendar in some subjects and behind in others. You must be sure to cover whatever material is necessary for them to pass the standardized tests on schedule. You might have to adjust your curriculum on the fly to ensure all subjects are adequately covered. So, be prepared to make changes. It's perfectly fine to keep improving your curriculum along the way. You don't have to "get it right" on the first try.

You don't have to do everything in advance

You need not build out lesson plans all the way from kindergarten through high school before you begin homeschooling. As long as you are prepared at the time a given lesson comes up, you will be fine. You can prepare lesson plans a few weeks in advance all the way from K through 12.

Consider a family with three children separated in age by a total of 6 years. If you teach K through 12 at home, you'll be teaching for 19 years. If you built a complete, detailed curriculum on Day One, some parts of it would be obsolete by the time the first child advanced four or five years into the program. The situation would worsen year by year for all three children.

There's just no value in working in detail far ahead. Things change. There are scientific and engineering advances. New literature and music are written. New discoveries are made about the past, and living history marches on. Prepare lesson plans a few weeks or months in advance, so the material will be up to date and still fresh in your mind when each lesson comes up.

Do you need a curriculum at all?

You will find comments on the Internet from homeschoolers who claim a curriculum is not necessary. Bear in mind these homeschoolers may have a different notion of what a curriculum is. When they say you don't need one, they don't actually mean you don't need anything at all. It seems unlikely a person could "wing it" for 12 years of teaching, even if they were very smart and also an experienced teacher.

A case in point: The site, Homeschool Story, offers a wide range of homeschooling resources and advice. Some of the articles have titles like "Why You Don't Need Science Curriculum" and "Why You Don't Need History Curriculum." When you read the material, you find she is not thinking of "curriculum" in the sense we've defined it here.

Instead, she suggests children can explore science concepts through play and hands-on investigation, and they can read about history from what she calls "real books" - possibly in preference to standard school textbooks. That is very good advice.

Her homeschool curriculum is loose - a general plan rather than a set of specific lesson plans. As we've mentioned elsewhere, that may work for you. Some people need more detail and organization than others.

You will definitely need at least a general roadmap or plan for guiding your children's development. Some people can work with a general plan, and others will do better if they have a comprehensive and detailed curriculum that includes lesson plans. Only you can decide how much structure you need to support your children adequately.

What about unexpected "teachable moments?"

By all means, take advantage of opportunities when they arise in the normal course of everyday life. It isn't necessary to have a formal lesson plan for every little thing that might happen. But for the overall educational process spanning 12 or 13 years, leaving everything to chance is unlikely to be effective.

Multiple teaching approaches

Even given a lot of structure and pre-built material, you cannot just follow a curriculum like a cookbook. You have to be able to adapt your teaching approach to suit each learner.

For instance, you might think mathematics will be a straightforward subject to teach, as there is nothing political or religious about it. But different individuals relate to mathematics in different ways. Some have a natural aptitude for it, and others will always struggle. You may need to learn multiple approaches to teaching mathematics and make adjustments for each child. Even if you are strong in mathematics, your children may not learn the subject in the same way as you did, or relate to it in the same terms as you do.

As you investigate commercial curriculum packages, you will find many that focus on a Christian education. Some of these are very well done, as Christianity is the majority religion in the US and tends to attract relatively affluent followers, thus creating a market for well-designed homeschool curricula. You may wish to draw from them even if you are not Christian. Always review the lesson plans, reading material, and exercises and make whatever edits you consider necessary to present the material in the way you prefer.

Conversely, religious parents may wish to add their perspective to material they find in generic curriculum packages. You cannot necessarily follow whatever is given in a commercial package without thinking about it and possibly modifying the material.

When a curriculum is not a curriculum

Many websites exist that claim to provide a free homeschool curriculum. In reality, they have collected and organized a number of Internet links to sites they do not control where they found lesson plans or other information relevant to building a curriculum (not unlike this site). A list of links is not a "curriculum." If you are looking for a pre-built curriculum, expect to pay for it.
