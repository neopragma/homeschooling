Challenges for Homeschoolers

Dave Nicolette

Proponents of homeschooling tend to gloss over or ignore the logistical, financial, and personal challenges inherent in homeschooling. Parents considering homeschooling should be aware of the potential downsides so they can make thoughtful decisions about whether to teach their own children, and determine how to prepare themselves to do so.

The article, "We'll Tell You the Reasons Why Homeschooling Is a Bad Idea" on the AptParenting website lists a number of challenges faced by homeschooling parents and their children. The article presents these issues as reasons not to homeschool. They want to convince you not to do it.

In my opinion, the issues raised in the article are realistic considerations for prospective homeschoolers, but not necessarily reasons to avoid homeschooling. It is important to understand what challenges you may face as a homeschooling parent so that you can take steps to overcome them; or to decide you cannot overcome them after all.

The authors of the article seem to assume, implicitly, that whatever public schools currently do represents the standard against which homeschooling must be measured. They describe challenges homeschoolers face when attempting to duplicate what the public schools do.

It seems to me that homeschoolers need not try to duplicate the educational experience of a conventional school. They can do better than a conventional school, depending on how they approach it. For some of the issues raised in the article, the answer may be to use an alternative approach, different from the conventional way, rather than to try and do exactly the same things that happen in a school.

The authors also seem to make the assumption that the condition of your home will be the same as it is when your children attend school outside the home. They are comparing the opportunities for learning and participating in activities that exist in the school building with those that exist in a standard middle-class house. But if you set things up to support homeschooling, your house and your life will be organized differently, so this is an apples-to-oranges comparison.

Let's step through the points made in the article and see whether they are meaningful, and if so whether they describe reasons not to homeschool categorically, or merely challenges homeschoolers must consider when deciding to homeschool and when planning their homeschool program.

Absence of Certified Educational Professionals

In public schools and any accredited charter or private schools, the teachers hold professional certifications that they have earned through university study and/or teacher training programs, and that they must maintain through ongoing education and teacher in-service days. As in any occupation, there is a bell curve of skills - most teachers are "average," some are very good, and some really ought to consider a different line of work.

But at least you can be assured all of them have passed a minimal skills assessment, and many of them have gone well beyond that level. Once certified, teachers must maintain their certification through continuing education and training.

You can expect them to know how to manage a room full of kids for several hours, and how to present curriculum material that has been prepared by someone else to an audience of children of average intelligence and without special needs. The best teachers can do far more than that, but you cannot realistically expect more from the majority of teachers.

Certification alone does not guarantee anything. With certification, many teachers are still not particularly good at the job. Lacking certification, many committed and hard-working adults can teach their children effectively. Yet, on the whole, certified professionals are generally better at any given type of work than people who have not gone through similar training and assessment.

A key factor the article fails to mention is the teacher-student ratio. This has a profound effect on the quality of teaching, notwithstanding the individual qualifications of each teacher or parent. In a typical public school class, the teacher-student ratio is in the range of 1:25 to 1:45, with every student coming from a different family background and a unique life situation. It is all but impossible for the teacher to give each student adequate one-on-one attention.

In a homeschooling setting, the teacher-student ratio is in the range of 1:1 to 1:8, typically representing between 1 and 3 families whose general life situations are similar, or at least well known to the adults in the teaching role. Even lacking professional teaching certification, a well-rounded, educated adult can help this small number of children learn more effectively than anyone could do with a group of 25 to 45 students.

Combining small class size, broadly similar life situations, and pedagogical methods that engage the children's natural propensity to help each other, the effectiveness of a well-organized homeschool system can be far greater than that of any public school - but this does not happen automatically.

The question for you - unless you happen to be a certified teacher yourself - is whether and how you can close the gap between what an educated adult can do and what a full-time professional teacher can do. Professional teachers enjoy a lot of support from the school system, professional associations, and fellow teachers that you will not have. Your support group will consist of other homeschoolers. You can do this, but don't underestimate the challenges.

The Internet is an excellent resource for you to find information about pedagogical methods and how to support special needs children. As you learn more about these things, you will be able to judge whether you can handle homeschooling and what further education you may require yourself to support your children's development properly.

The article seeks to convince you not to homeschool, so it omits certain information. In particular, the authors assume you will be working alone. That will be the case only if you so choose. Homeschoolers find each other and team up to support one another's needs. If you are good at teaching English but not so strong in Science, you can find another homeschooler in your town who is good at teaching Science and not so strong in History, and one who is good at History and not so strong in English. The three of you can cover a lot of territory for all your children.

Through self-education, teaming up with other homeschoolers, engaging older children to help teach younger ones, and exploiting alternative pedagogical methods, it is feasible to close the gap with certified teachers. This is a real consideration you will have to deal with, but it is not a show-stopper. Only you can decide whether you can do everything necessary to succeed.

Special Needs May Not Be Met

The article points out that "some children require special learning aids and tutors, and cannot be home-schooled." It is true that some children require special learning aids and a specialized approach to teaching, but I resist the conclusion this automatically makes homeschooling impossible for them.

Many special needs children do not fare well in standardized settings that are optimized for the "middle of the pack," and many certified, professional teachers are ill-prepared to support them. There is no one in the world who knows your special needs child as well as you do, or who cares about them as much as you do. Techniques for helping children with dyslexia, attention deficit disorder, autism, physical limitations, or other challenges are learnable; "special learning aids" are obtainable; and specially-trained tutors can be hired.

Despite special training for the teachers, public schools are notoriously bad at helping any child who falls outside the statistical norm. A child who is a little slow or a little fast, a little shy or a little energetic, who falls anywhere on the autism spectrum, or who has difficulty speaking, hearing, seeing, or writing is likely to have a miserable experience in a public school, and to face an uphill battle to learn anything at all.

Homeschool Burnout

The article warns us that parents may become exhausted from long hours of teaching on top of all the other demands of life.

The authors of the article assume you will spend many hours per day teaching, just as a classroom teacher in school does. In the lower grades, the same students stay with a teacher all day in the same room. In higher grades, students still stay in school all day, but move from room to room and from teacher to teacher. The teacher's day-to-day work experience is that they must manage a classroom several hours a day; a single room in the lower grades, and multiple class periods in the upper grades. Most teachers work considerable overtime without pay, grading students' work and preparing lesson plans. But homeschoolers need not have a similar experience.

The one-way lecture format typically used in classrooms may not be (and probably is not) the most effective way to teach. Many schools and homeschoolers use the Montessori Method, based on seven principles that appear to be quite effective - and quite different from the conventional classroom format. Another concept that has proven useful is Flipped Learning. An approach particularly useful for learning hands-on skills is Experiential Learning. These and other techniques are listed under Pedagogical Methods. There are specific techniques for helping special needs children learn, as well, such as this advice for teaching dyslexic children.

The point here is that homeschooling need not consist of having the children sit in a chair all day long while you lecture them. There are methods of learning that provide very good results in less time, and some of them are especially effective with small groups as opposed to large classes. It is not necessary to force children to learn. It is human nature for children to absorb whatever knowledge and skills they can. A teacher's job is mainly to enable this, and to avoid suppressing or discouraging it.

It is very possible you can guide your children's education effectively in 2 to 4 hours per day rather than 6 or 7, as in school. You will also have 2 to 4 hours of daily preparation. That is more-or-less a standard work day, only half of which involves directly teaching. It is almost certainly less stressful than the working life of a professional teacher in a school. It is not a burnout situation - but it is also not a part-time job.

Shifting Priorities

It seems to me that if you have chosen the homeschooling path, it is because you have made your children the top priority (assuming your motivations are positive - see Why Homeschool?). Other aspects of your life are of lower priority, and that is by choice. As long as the children remain your top priority, then guiding their development is likely to be the most rewarding thing you could possibly do with your time.

On the other hand, if you do start to burn out, or if you do change your priorities, or you realize your motivation really is negative and homeschooling just isn't working, then the fall-back position is to send your children to school. To be prepared for that possibility, the logical thing to do is to follow your State's curriculum guidelines so that the children will be at the same general level across all subject areas as the students who were taught in school all along. That will help them assimilate into the school routine without having to catch up with their peers.

One point the authors make is that your career may not progress as well as you would like because you are spending so much time doing homeschooling. In the modern world, and especially post-pandemic, most people do not find the old style of workaholism to be satisfying. White-collar workers are insisting that employers allow alternatives to sitting in an office all day long, without curtailing career growth opportunities. Blue-collar workers, especially skilled workers who work as contractors, are choosing to balance work and free time in a more flexible way than traditionally.

It is more feasible than ever for you to weave homeschooling and work into your life with a minimum of stress, and without limiting your career prospects. That is because of (not in spite of) the way in which priorities are shifting generally throughout society.

Costs

The article lists "high costs" as a reason not to homeschool, but in reality the costs will not be excessive. The authors list textbooks, stationery, a computer, Internet service, educational software, seating arrangement, field trips, audio-visual aids, hobby classes, sports, library fees, legal fees, and support group membership fees.

Expect to purchase some textbooks and to rearrange some furniture, but generally these costs are no different than they would be without homeschooling. You will incur additional costs for things like pre-built curriculum packages, supplies and materials, software, and educational games. When you send your children to public or private schools, those things are included. They are paid for with your taxes or private school fees. You will still be paying the same taxes, while also having to purchase certain things yourself. So, there are some extra expenses, but they need not be excessive.

In general, textbooks cost more than other kinds of books. A quick Internet search for terms like "used textbooks" will return many links to companies that buy and sell used textbooks or that offer textbooks on a rental basis or on a lending library basis.

Educational institutions often require a specific textbook for a given course. For public schools or state-accredited private schools, this is due to the state's selection of textbooks; for colleges and universities, it's more commonly because the professor specifies a particular book.

As a homeschooler, you need not conform to this. Many books contain similar information, and you can choose any book(s) you consider relevant to the learning goals of your children. You need not pay $150 for an "official" textbook when an equivalent book costing $25 is available, or when you can buy the official textbook third-hand for $25.

A typical middle-class family in the US already has one or more computers, already has Internet service, already spends money on "stuff" for the kids (stationery, Lego® bricks, whiteboards, and many other things that may be used for recreation, education, or both), already spends money on supplies for things like science fairs, already takes their kids on field trips, already signs their kids up for youth sports and other activities, and already supports their children's hobbies. These costs are not "extra" for homeschoolers.

A good deal of educational software is free, but you may elect to purchase some software products that offer specific benefits. Besides, it isn't the software that provides the education; it's the content the children study when using the software. Internet sites and freeware are available for most such purposes. You may not have to purchase any software at all, although you might find some products to be convenient.

People generally use the Internet instead of the public library. Even if you prefer the library, membership is probably not a heavy expense. After all, the mission of a public library is to make books accessible to as many people as possible. They typically do not charge exorbitant fees.

You won't have any legal fees specific to homeschooling; it is unclear why the authors of the article mentioned this. Your State may require a registration fee when you file the paperwork, but it will be small. It is possible this is what the authors meant by "legal fees." In any case, you do not need to hire a lawyer to help you set up homeschooling. The procedures are not that complicated.

Some homeschooling support groups are free and others are cheap. Homeschoolers combine forces to form sports teams, and depending on your state's laws your children may be eligible to participate in public school sports programs, as well. If not, then most cities and towns have community youth sports leagues of various kinds. Your family probably already pays the annual fee to use community centers and sports fields in your town, and would do so even if your children attended school outside the home. If they play certain popular sports like soccer or softball, they are probably already participating in city leagues.

When it comes to group activities like dance or martial arts, you probably already pay the fees for your children to be enrolled in commercial programs. For music, they can join the same community bands and orchestras as adult community members do, rather than playing in the school band or orchestra. Many larger Christian churches have their own orchestras, made up of congregation members. Some states may allow homeschooled children to participate in a public school band, as well.

For drama, the kids can join the local community theater group. If you live in or near a large city, there may be theatre organizations for children, as well.

Social Pressure

The article warns that friends and acquaintences of the parents may put pressure on them to return their kids to regular schools. I think this is a non-issue. It is no one else's business how you raise your children. Anyone who criticizes you for choosing homeschooling is a fair-weather friend indeed. Find new friends among other homeschoolers and among open-minded people generally.

Isolation

The article states children "who are home-schooled may feel lonely, friendless and isolated, especially so if they don't have siblings." This is under the parents' control. If you keep the children at home as if in prison, then it is certainly true they will not have the opportunity to interact with other children their own age.

Some solutions to this problem have already been mentioned under the heading of cost. There are ample opportunities for your children to interact with others, but you have to take the initiative to find these opportunities and connect with them. The difference with a regular school is that the school organizes social activities (such as school dances), sports (such as the football team), and extra-curricular activities (such as chess club or science club) and provides an environment where the students interact routinely all day. As a homeschooler, that responsibility falls to you.

Speaking of school dances - in most cases, kids can attend a dance even if they don't attend the school that is hosting the dance. It's common for groups of friends to go together, and there's probably no requirement that they all attend the same school. Older kids often take a date to school dances, and their date needn't attend the same school.

In addition to community activities organized by your city recreation department, homeowners association, neighborhood association, or religious organization - which often include teen dances, Halloween parties, holiday celebrations, and other social events - you can join with other homeschoolers to support special-interest clubs and sports teams and to organize events like mini-Olympics, concerts, plays, and dance recitals.

Your church, synagogue, mosque, or temple may sponsor youth groups of various kinds, often including music, art, dance, and sports. If you live near an observatory, planetarium, zoo, aquarium, or children's science museum, your homeschooled children have the same opportunities to participate in youth programs organized by those facilities as do the children who attend school.

Another opportunity for social interaction comes from teaming up with other homeschoolers to cross-teach children from multiple households.

The fact a child does not go to school every day does not mean they never leave the house at all, and never see any other people.

Hindered Development

The authors underscore their concern about social interaction under this heading in the article. They say that in a traditional school, students are exposed to others from diverse cultural backgrounds. In reality, this is rarely the case. Most public schools draw their student population from the area surrounding the school, and neighborhoods in the US tend to be culturally homogeneous. Private schools typically seek students from the same general socioeconomic background, and are even less diverse than public schools.

On top of that, the students themselves tend to congregate into like-minded groups or cliques within the school. Even if a school has a diverse student population on paper, the direct experience of any given student is that they are surrounded by people just like themselves...by choice.

In the past, schools have used "busing" to bring diversity to the student body, but even in those days the children who were brought in from far away went back to their home neighborhoods at the end of each school day. There was little meaningful interaction between cultural groups, and virtually no opportunity for bussed students to participate in extracurricular activities.

In my view, this is another aspect of development that is the responsibility of the homeschooling parents. In seeking activities and groups for your children to participate in, you have the opportunity to look for diversity in those groups. You can even be the one who creates such a group. You are in control. The fact your child is schooled at home does not deprive him/her of the opportunity to meet different kinds of people. Even if they are in school, you may have to take extra steps to ensure they can meet and interact with people different from themselves, because the student population is probably culturally homogeneous; so this is not an additional task for you as a homeschooler.

I must point out, too, that many homeschoolers choose this path because they want to limit their children's exposure to other cultures, races, and religions. For example, a white supremacist Christian extremist would not want their children to interact with non-believers or people of color. Their children's development is "hindered" from the perspective of the authors of the article, but quite possibly not from the parents' perspective. (I'm not here to judge - just to point out the factors to consider.)

Assessment Pressure

The article states that students attending public schools take assessment tests at the end of each year to progress to the next grade, and that teachers periodically give them similar tests throughout the school term to help them prepare. There seems to be an assumption that homeschoolers would not do the same.

I think this is a non-issue, as any responsible homeschooling parent will help their children prepare for standardized tests. In fact, by using alternative pedagogical methods that are generally more effective than the lecture-style approach used in public schools, the children will be far better prepared to pass the periodic assessment tests than attendees at public schools.

Assessments vary quite a lot from State to State. The tests in New York or Connecticut are more demanding than those in Alabama or Mississippi. Homeschoolers in States that make relatively light demands on students will have no difficulty ensuring their children are able to pass the official assessment tests. In fact, it is likely they chose homeschooling so that their children could surpass the results usually achieved by the public schools in their State. These children will find the official assessments easy.

Most assessments are multiple-choice. This makes machine-grading a large number of tests easy and fast. There are well-documented techniques to improve the odds of "guessing" the answer to a multiple-choice question, when you are not sure of the correct answer. Your child's everyday experience with this kind of "test" can be in the nature of play, and they will not consider it stressful to take the "real" assessment test when the time comes.

There is quite a lot of criticism of standardized testing. In a 2011 article entitled "Whoever Said There's No Such Thing As a Stupid Question Never Looked Carefully at a Standardized Test", Alfie Kohn debunks the idea that the questions on standardized tests are well-crafted or that the test results signify anything meaningful about the student's level of knowledge or future prospects.

The late Linda McNeil, co-founder of the Center for Education at Rice University, wrote about the damage to students' learning that occurs when teachers alter their lessons to target standardized assessment tests instead of real learning. She writes that a science teacher she interviewed said, "You really have to unteach some things when you get to the proficiencies. That's what I have found. 'Forget these expansions into things. Go for the simple things.' And we defeat our purpose in the sense that if we teach creative thinking, if we teach analytical thinking, and if we teach children to look beyond the obvious into the not-quite-so-obvious, then the proficiency test comes and they think analytically, think beyond the obvious, and they will get them [the proficiency test answers] wrong because they know all of the exceptions to the rules and they say 'but what if...?' And so I try to reserve at least a week prior to the proficiencies and throw everything aside and they check in their books and we close down the school and we give them the proficiency drills."

I'd like to share an anecdote, although it's a bit dated. It comes from around 1990. A professional teacher who had many years' experience teaching in Massachussetts became certified in Texas after she and her husband moved there. She'd been teaching in Texas several years, as well. When the state instituted standardized assessment tests at specific grade levels, they required all the teachers to take the same assessments as a way to ensure they were reasonable.

The reaction of this individual, and according to her the teachers' union as well, was to push back against the requirement. She complained that the test was too hard. In fact, some percentage of the professional teachers failed it. I don't recall what the percentage was; it was not high, but then again why should any professional teacher fail a sixth-grade assessment? She showed me a copy of the assessment. It was dead simple. A fourth-grader could have passed it effortlessly on the first try.

Don't tell me you can't do better than that at home. You can.

The idea, after Kohn, that the assessments are not objectively meaningful, and the idea, after McNeil, that teachers must "unteach" things to make their students stupid enough to pass the assessments, are not show-stoppers for homeschooling. The skill of taking standardized tests is just another life skill, like riding a bicycle, filling out a tax return, applying for a job, cooking a meal, or replacing a car battery. You can teach your children to pass your state's standardized assessment without compromising their actual education. The assessments are not difficult; passing them is just another box to check on the way to a diploma.

Lack of Competition

Finally, the article suggests homeschooled children will not be well prepared to face the "fierce competition" they will have to deal with in real life, because they have not had to compete with other students in school.

One problem with this statement is the underlying assumption that competition between students is universally good. A degree of competition, under controlled conditions such as sports events or musical auditions, is positive. But when students feel as if they must "defeat" one another academically in order to succeed in life, the result is more often low self-esteem, low confidence, difficulty with collaboration, and an inability to handle setbacks gracefully.

While cut-throat academic competition is the norm in some countries, notably South Korea and Japan, this is not the case in the US. It is necessary for students to learn certain subjects up to a certain level in order to qualify for university entry, but we do not have suicide-inducing competitive pressure.

Another problem with the statement is the same error the authors make in previous comments - the assumption that a homeschooled child never sees the world outside their parents' house. There are ample opportunities for positive experiences in competitive situations, through community-sponsored or church-sponsored youth sports leagues and other activities. The children will attend summer camps and summer development programs, and play with other children on weekends throughout the year. They are not kept in a sealed box.

What about that fierce competition in the adult world? There is a lot of truth to the adage that 90% of success in any job is just to show up. With some exceptions, there really is very little "fierce competition" out there. The truth is that most people are not very good at their work, and one need only raise the bar a little to be seen as an exceptional employee. Lack of competition is not something for homeschoolers to worry about.

Conclusion

The challenges presented in the article are real, but they are not show-stoppers for homeschooling. They are simply considerations that homeschooling parents must take into account when deciding whether to homeschool in the first place, and if so, how to mitigate the potential negative effects, if any. It is important to go into this with eyes open, but not with eyes wide in fear.
