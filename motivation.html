Main page => Motivation

Motivated learners will benefit more from your teaching and guidance than unmotivated learners, and they will enjoy the homeschooling experience more, too. Motivated learners will be easier to manage and guide through the curriculum, and to keep on track with your state's educational standards.

Keeping your learners motivated will require mindful effort on your part; there are no "standard" answers. You need information in three key areas, as well as patience and a willingness to adapt based on how your learners respond to your motivational efforts.

The three key areas to learn about are:

  1. How human motivation works
  2. Understanding your learners
  3. Techniques that others have found effective

How human motivation works

Our understanding of human motivation is constantly changing as researchers and practitioners advance their knowledge. Currently, the leading theory of motivation is called Self-Determination Theory, or SDT. Here is a summary of the six levels of motivation (or self-determination) described in SDT, as documented in the article Motivation & Self-Determination Theory by Mike Bowler.

Amotivated. No desire to do it. If it happens at all then it was an accident or a side effect of something else.

External. "I was told to do it" and there are likely external punishments and/or rewards involved.
Ed. - This is where positive and negative reinforcement get you. There will be times when this is the only way to get your learners moving, but if you depend on this all the time you will quickly become exhausted.

Introjected. Internalized nagging. "I feel that I should do it to meet my own goals." This is driven by self-control and internal rewards or punishments.
Ed. - This may apply to older learners, but is unlikely to come into play with younger ones.

Identified. "Doing this will help me achieve my goals." I may not care about this activity for itself but doing it will move me closer to a goal that I do care about.

Integrated. Doing this is part of who I am. "I volunteer at a food bank because I am kind." Behavior tied to our identity.

Intrinsic. "I love this - it feels great." Enjoyable for its own sake.

SDT treats External, Introjected, Identified, and Integrated as forms of extrinsic motivation. Ideally, we would like learners to be intrinsically motivated for all academic and skill-oriented activities, but as a practical matter Identified and Integrated motivation are powerful as well.

The popular book by Daniel Pink, Drive: The Surprising Truth About What Motivates Us, the author summarizes the results of a number of formal studies in an engaging way. Three key elements fall out from these studies: Autonomy, Mastery, and Purpose.

In this context, Autonomy means a desire to be self-directed rather than being told what to do. Autonomy is also identified in SDT as a strong driver of intrinsic motivation. Mastery means the desire to become better at a skill or more knowledgeable about a subject. Purpose refers to connecting what we are doing with some larger purpose in a way that is meaningful or rewarding.

Understanding your learners

Individuals are motivated or demotivated by different factors, including (but not limited to)

  • Age or developmental stage
  • Sex
  • Neurodiversity
  • Emotional issues
  • Health issues
  • Physical challenges
  • Learner's relationship with you

When you are working with more than one learner, the picture quickly becomes complex. Additional factors can affect the dynamics of the learning environment in ways that motivate or demotivate each individual, such as

  • Age differences - learners at different academic levels and developmental stages
  • Sex - two boys interact differently than two girls, and differently than one of each
  • Competition for your attention/approval
  • Behavioral issues whose causes you don't know, when some of your learners are not your own children
  • Neurodiversity - how learners interact with each other vs. when they are alone

Professional teachers receive training in classroom management as well as some basic education in psychology. Even so, many struggle with these issues. As a homeschooler who may not have been trained for this, you must be prepared for some friction and be ready to practice patience.

Techniques that others have found effective

The question of motivation may seem daunting, but in fact education researchers, professional teachers, and homeschoolers have been facing this challenge for a very long time. An Internet search or a query to your favorite AI assistant will turn up a long list of suggestions. Ultimately, it's up to you to tailor these suggestions to your own situation. Here are a few.

Choice and autonomy

The studies Daniel Pink refers to in Drive and the motivational theory Self-Determination Theory all identify autonomy as a major driver of intrinsic motivation. Of course, you are guiding your learners through a curriculum, so they can't do literally anything they please, but with the curriculum as "guardrails" you can give learners a certain amount of autonomy.

Learners can participate in weekly lesson planning. They can choose the order in which to work through the planned units. They can choose between different options for completing an assignment, such as writing, drawing, or creating a video.

Some of the pedagogical methods we describe on this site include learner autonomy as part of their operational model, especially Montessori, Charlotte Mason, Flipped Learning, and the 5 C's.

Interest-Led Learning

You can often find a way to build lesson plans around each learner's personal interests. For instance, when a young child is learning to read, exactly what they read isn't so important; they're practicing the skill of reading as such. So, if they like outer space, they can read about astronomy and space exploration; if they like animals, they can read about animals. Arithmetic is part and parcel of many areas of life, so you can often build a lesson plan around some activity or topic the learner is already motivated to learn about. If an older student is developing critical thinking skills, they can work with material about any subject that interests them.

Timed Sessions

One of the main criticisms of conventional classroom teaching is that students must spend long periods of time sitting still and being quiet while working on the same things in the same way over and over again. As a homeschooler, you have full control over the pacing and scheduling of educational activities.

Consider breaking up study sessions in a way that alternates between different kinds of activity. You can adapt the Pomodoro Technique to homeschooling. That's a method of time management many adults use in their work. It involves a period of focused work (typically 25 minutes) followed by a period of rest or some alternative activity (typically 5 minutes). You can adjust those time periods as appropriate to your situation and your learners.

Hands-on Learning

We've already mentioned the problem of having students sit still for long periods of time. Including hands-on learning activities as part of the daily routine will help alleviate the boredom of sitting still, and will encourage intrinsic motivation.

Hands-on learning can take many different forms. It can involve anything from baking cookies to building a "maker" kit (robot, weather station, etc.) to taking a field trip. It's often feasible (and quite easy) to incorporate academic content into hands-on activities, especially mathematics and science content.

We've included a few links to resources for hands-on learning supplies, and what professional teachers call "manipulatives," on the page about Manipulatives, Builder Kits and Sets, Maker Projects.

Gamification

The term gamification refers to making work-related or educational activities "game-like" as a way to maintain people's engagement. This can include games specifically designed for education, general games that are suitable to include in lesson plans, and organizing activities that are not "games" in ways that feel like games. There are lists of board games, card games, online challenges, and video games on the Teaching and Learning page.

Besides those, you can find phone apps, computer apps, or online apps that are game-like and usable for educational purposes. An example is the phone/online app DuoLingo for learning foreign languages. It keeps people engaged by making the experience game-like. You can probably find many more apps that were not necessarily intended for education, but can be used that way.

Breaking the routine

We're advised to establish a routine for homeschooling and stick to it, so there is consistency and predictability. Sometimes deviating from the routine can refresh learners' interest and motivation. This can be very simple, like holding class outdoors instead of indoors, or reversing the usual sequence of activities.

One-on-one attention

One reason people prefer small class sizes in schools is that it enables the teacher to interact one-on-one with the students. In a homeschool setting you probably have between 1 and 8 learners, so one-on-one interaction should be quite feasible. Learners appreciate the focused attention and help, and it can drive motivation.

Community activities

Including your learners with other homeschool groups, clubs and events organized by the city or local churches, and similar opportunities can foster higher motivation by enabling cooperative and competitive interaction with children beyond the immediate homeschool group. Some suggestions are included on the page about Challenges for Homeschoolers.

Giving rewards for achievement is a good way to maintain motivation, although this form of motivation is likely to be extrinsic and may only extend to the External level in the SDT model.

Learners as teachers

When you have different age groups in your homeschool, one way to build intrinsic motivation is to have the older students mentor and guide the younger ones. Both age groups tend to appreciate this, and to feel more engaged with the experience. Some of the pedagogical methods we describe on this site have this idea baked into their operating models.
